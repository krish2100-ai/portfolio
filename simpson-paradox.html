Simpson’s Paradox

A reverse in trend caused by hidden group structure.

Simpson’s paradox is a statistical phenomenon in which a trend appears in separate groups of data but reverses or disappears when the groups are combined. It is a classic reminder that aggregated averages can be misleading when the underlying groups differ in size or composition.

In its simplest form, a comparison between two groups can look favorable in one direction when analyzed within subgroups, yet reverse at the aggregate level. This happens because the subgroup mix changes between the groups being compared. In many applied settings, this is exactly the sort of pitfall that can arise in experiment analysis, medical studies, or business reporting.

Why it happens

The main driver is confounding. If one group has a larger share of observations from a subgroup with a systematically different outcome rate, then the aggregate comparison may be dominated by composition rather than by the comparison itself. Once the data are split into more meaningful subpopulations, the original pattern may disappear or reverse.

A worked example: two doctors

Suppose we compare two doctors on their surgery success rates. Each treats a mix of difficult and easy cases, but their case mixes are very different.

Case type Doctor A Doctor B
Difficult cases 6 / 10 = 60% 1 / 5 = 20%
Easy cases 10 / 10 = 100% 90 / 100 = 90%

Notice that Doctor A is better in both categories: 60% vs 20% on difficult cases and 100% vs 90% on easy cases.

Overall success rate

Now combine each doctor’s cases into a single overall rate:

Doctor Total successes Overall rate
Doctor A (6 + 10) / (10 + 10) = 16 / 20 80.0%
Doctor B (1 + 90) / (5 + 100) = 91 / 105 86.7%

The paradox appears: by the overall rate, Doctor B (86.7%) looks more successful than Doctor A (80.0%) — even though Doctor A was better in every individual category. This happens because Doctor B mostly handled easy cases (100 of 105), which inflates the combined average.

Adjusting for case mix

The fair way to compare is to hold the case mix fixed and re-weight each doctor’s category rates. This is called standardization.

Using Doctor A’s case mix (50% difficult, 50% easy)

DoctorWeighted rateResult
Doctor A 0.5 × 60% + 0.5 × 100% 80.0%
Doctor B 0.5 × 20% + 0.5 × 90% 55.0%

Under Doctor A’s mix, Doctor A wins, 80.0% vs 55.0%.

Using Doctor B’s case mix (5/105 difficult, 100/105 easy)

DoctorWeighted rateResult
Doctor A (5/105) × 60% + (100/105) × 100% 98.1%
Doctor B (5/105) × 20% + (100/105) × 90% 86.7%

Under Doctor B’s mix, Doctor A still wins, 98.1% vs 86.7%.

Why it matters

The decision flips depending on how we measure:

  • Raw overall rates: Doctor B looks better (86.7% vs 80.0%).
  • Mix-adjusted (either mix): Doctor A is consistently better.

This is the heart of Simpson’s paradox: an unadjusted aggregate can point to the wrong winner because the two groups face different case mixes. Doctor B’s higher overall number came from treating mostly easy cases, not from being a better doctor. Once we control for difficulty, the true picture — that Doctor A is stronger in every category — is restored.

In data science and experimentation, this is why we cannot trust aggregate metrics without checking for group-level structure. It is especially important in A/B testing, observational studies, and fairness analyses, where a hidden confounder can reverse the apparent story. A good analyst always asks: are we comparing apples to apples, or is the result driven by composition?
